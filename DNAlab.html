    Overview

    In this virtual lab you will pull DNA out of fruit cells, learn how DNA fingerprints are made, load and run an agarose gel, and use your gel to solve two cases: a crime scene and a paternity test.

    Background

    Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is a double-stranded helix built from nucleotides. Each nucleotide has a nitrogenous base (adenine, thymine, guanine, or cytosine), the sugar deoxyribose, and a phosphate group. Phosphodiester bonds link the phosphate of one nucleotide to the sugar of the next, forming the sugar–phosphate backbone. Hydrogen bonds between A–T and G–C hold the two strands together.

    Every phosphate in the backbone carries a negative charge. Keep that in mind: it explains why salt helps us extract DNA and why DNA moves through a gel.

    Cells protect their DNA. Eukaryotic DNA sits inside a membrane-bound nucleus, and plant cells add a tough cell wall. To get DNA out, we break those barriers with physical and chemical means. To examine the DNA, we use gel electrophoresis, which separates DNA fragments by size.

      PCR in Brief: Making Millions of Copies

      A crime scene might leave only a few skin cells under a fingernail. A nasal swab might hold only a few virus particles. That isn't enough DNA to see. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) fixes that by copying one chosen stretch of DNA over and over.

      PCR uses a heat-stable DNA polymerase, free nucleotides, and two short primers. The primers are built to base-pair on either side of the region we want, so only the DNA between the primers is copied. Each cycle has three temperature steps, and each cycle doubles the number of copies.

      1. Denature · 95 °C
      Heat separates the two DNA strands.
      2. Anneal · ~55–60 °C
      Primers base-pair to each single strand on either side of the target.
      3. Extend · 72 °C
      Polymerase builds a new strand starting at each primer.

      Start: 10 copies of a target region from a few skin cells.

      At a crime scene

      Forensic labs use PCR to copy the STR regions (next section) from tiny amounts of blood, skin, or saliva. The copies are then sorted by size, like in our gel, to make a DNA profile.

      In a COVID test

      SARS-CoV-2 has an RNA genome, so the test first uses reverse transcriptase to copy the viral RNA into DNA (RT-PCR). PCR then copies a viral gene. A fluorescent probe glows more with each cycle, so the machine reads the result in real time. A positive sample crosses the glow threshold early, and a negative sample never does.

      STRs and VNTRs: What Makes a DNA Fingerprint

      About 99.9% of your DNA is identical to everyone else's. DNA fingerprinting focuses on the few places that vary a lot between people.

      At many places on our chromosomes, a short DNA sequence repeats end-to-end, like GATA GATA GATA GATA…. Everyone has these tandem repeats in the same places, but the number of repeats differs from person to person.

      You have two copies of each chromosome: one from your mother and one from your father. So at each repeat site (locus) you have two alleles, which may have different repeat numbers. When PCR primers sit on either side of the repeats, a longer allele gives a longer PCR product, and that shows up as a band in a different place on the gel.

      How Gel Electrophoresis Works

      Electrophoresis means "carrying with electricity." An electric field pulls DNA through a slab of agarose gel, and the gel sorts the DNA by size.

      Why does DNA move?

      Each phosphate in DNA's backbone is negatively charged. When the power supply is on, the negative electrode (cathode, black) pushes DNA away and the positive electrode (anode, red) pulls it in. Students remember it as "DNA runs to red." That is why the wells are placed at the black end.

      Why do fragments separate?

      Agarose is a seaweed sugar that forms a tangled mesh of fibers full of tiny pores, like a sponge. Small fragments slip through the pores easily and travel far. Large fragments keep snagging and travel only a short way.

      A longer fragment has more charge, but it also has more mass, so every DNA molecule has about the same charge per unit of length. In open buffer, all DNA would move at the same speed. The gel mesh is what separates fragments by size.

      A more concentrated gel (for example 2%) has smaller pores. It separates small fragments better but nearly stops large ones.

      Case 2: A Paternity Test

      A mother wants to establish who the biological father of her child is. Two men, Alleged Father 1 (AF1) and Alleged Father 2 (AF2), agree to be tested. DNA from all four people was amplified by PCR at three loci (A, B, and C) and run on gels.

      The rule for reading a paternity gel

      A child gets one allele from the mother and one from the father at every locus. So:

      1. Find the child's band that matches a mother's band. That is the maternal allele.
      2. The child's other band is the paternal allele. It must come from the biological father.
      3. A man who lacks the paternal allele is excluded. A man who has it at every locus can't be excluded.
