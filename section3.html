Chapter 9: Cell Division and Sexual Reproduction

How one cell becomes two (mitosis), how gametes are made (meiosis), how cells decide to divide or die, and why cells age.

9.1 Key terms

For each term on your list, you should be able to describe it in just a few words. Press a card to flip it.

9.2 Interphase: getting ready to divide

“Prep” vs. interphase

In class you’ll hear this called “prep,” because the cell is preparing to divide. The scientific term is interphase (inter = between): the whole stretch of time between divisions. It is not a resting stage. During interphase the cell grows, does its normal job, copies its DNA, and gets ready. Use “interphase” on exams.

Interphase has three steps: G₁ (grow), S (synthesize, or copy, the DNA), and G₂ (grow more and get ready). Then comes M phase, where mitosis divides the nucleus and cytokinesis divides the cell. Choose a phase to explore it.

From the cell cycle lab

When you counted onion root tip cells, most were in interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase, so a slide catches most cells there. Copying all of the DNA and growing takes far longer than moving finished chromosomes around.

9.7 Telomeres and cell aging

If our cells can keep dividing, why do we age? Almost all body cells can divide only about 50 times (the Hayflick limit). Each time DNA is copied, a little bit is lost from the chromosome ends. Telomeres are repeating DNA caps on the ends that take this loss so the genes don’t. Telomerase is an enzyme that adds telomere DNA back.

9.8 Cancer: when division goes wrong

Cancer is a group of diseases involving abnormal cell growth. It is caused by mutations in DNA that disable the checkpoints and apoptosis.

1 · Checkpoints fail

Proteins that run the checkpoints come from tumor suppressor genes. p53 is the most commonly mutated tumor suppressor gene in all cancers.

2 · Apoptosis fails

The damaged cell should be destroyed, but it escapes programmed cell death and keeps dividing.

Bonus · Telomerase on

Cancer cells turn telomerase back on, so they never hit the Hayflick limit.

Chromosomes 1–22 are autosomes: perfectly matched pairs that look the same in males and females. Pair 23 is the sex chromosomes. In females they match (XX); in males they don’t (XY): the Y is much smaller and carries genes responsible for “maleness.”

10.3 Nondisjunction: when meiosis goes wrong

Nondisjunction is the failure of chromosomes to separate correctly during meiosis I (homologous chromosomes stay together) or meiosis II (sister chromatids stay together). The result is gametes with an extra chromosome (n + 1) or a missing one (n − 1). A zygote made from one of these has an abnormal chromosome number: aneuploidy. Follow chromosome 21 below.

10.4 Mendel, Punnett squares, and the test cross

10.5 Beyond simple dominance

Know these four patterns as two pairs that are easy to mix up.

Polygenic

Many genes → one trait. Produces continuous variation: a smooth range of phenotypes, like human height or skin color.

Pleiotropic

One gene → many effects. A defective cystic fibrosis gene causes mucus buildup in the lungs, pancreas, and liver, plus salty sweat. Sickle-cell anemia is another example.

Incomplete dominance

Blend: the heterozygote is in between. Red (CRCR) × white (CWCW) → pink (CRCW). F₂: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.

Codominance

Both show: both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote. Type AB blood shows both A and B.

10.6 Reading a pedigree

A pedigree is a family tree for a trait. Squares are males, circles are females, filled shapes have the trait, a horizontal line joins parents, and a vertical line leads to their children. This family tracks albinism.

10.7 The central dogma and mutations

The central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein. Genes in DNA are transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into a chain of amino acids that folds into a protein. Proteins shape the phenotype, so a change in DNA can change the protein and the trait. (More in Chapter 11.)

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. You can get them from errors in DNA replication, from mutagens (UV light, chemicals in cigarette smoke), or by inheriting a germ-line mutation from a parent. The three common types are base substitution, insertion, and deletion.

11.1 Base pairing and Chargaff’s rules

Complementary base pairs

A–T (adenine with thymine) and G–C (guanine with cytosine). A purine (A, G: double ring) always pairs with a pyrimidine (T, C: single ring), so the helix keeps a constant width.

Chargaff’s rules

In double-stranded DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C. So A + G = T + C = 50%, and all four add up to 100%.

How to solve a Chargaff problem
  1. Find the partner of the base you’re given; it has the same %.
  2. Add those two together and subtract from 100%.
  3. Split what’s left equally between the other two bases.

Example: 20% A → T = 20%. A + T = 40%. 100 − 40 = 60%. G = C = 30%.

11.3 The central dogma and transcription

The central dogma is the flow of information: DNA → RNA → protein. Using a gene to make a protein is gene expression, and it happens in two stages.

Transcription (nucleus)

  • RNA polymerase binds the promoter on one DNA strand.
  • It moves along the template and builds a complementary mRNA.
  • RNA uses U (uracil) instead of T.

Translation (cytoplasm)

  • The ribosome reads the mRNA three bases (one codon) at a time.
  • tRNA with the matching anticodon brings each amino acid.
  • Starts at AUG; ends at a stop codon.

11.4 Translation and the genetic code

Translation follows the genetic code: each codon (3 mRNA bases) stands for one amino acid. There are 64 codons; AUG (methionine) is the start codon, and UAA, UAG, and UGA are stop codons. The code is universal: all living things use it.
