During the 1860s, the Austrian monk Gregor Mendel worked out the basic rules of inheritance using garden peas. He succeeded because he followed one trait at a time, counted thousands of offspring, and kept careful records. He was also lucky: the pea traits he chose are each controlled by a single gene, with one allele completely dominant over the other.

Mendel's Law of Segregation says that each parent has two alleles for a gene, those two alleles separate when gametes form (so each gamete carries just one), and fertilization brings two alleles back together. In this lab you will model segregation with coins, test your results with a chi-square test, solve a human inheritance problem, identify unknown parents from their offspring, build a two-gene Punnett square, and trace a few traits through your own family.

Coins as gametes

Suppose tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t) in a plant. Cross two heterozygous plants: Tt × Tt. Each parent makes two kinds of gametes, T and t, in equal numbers, which is just like a coin landing heads or tails. Flipping two coins at once is like one gamete from each parent meeting at fertilization.

Heads = T, tails = t. The expected genotype ratio is 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, so out of 64 offspring you expect 16 TT, 32 Tt, and 16 tt.

Step B · How a chi-square test works

Your counts will almost never be exactly 16 : 32 : 16. The question is whether they are close enough that the difference could just be chance. Karl Pearson's chi-square (χ²) goodness-of-fit test answers that question. You compare what you observed (O) with what you expected (E), and turn the difference into one number.

We always start by assuming the expected ratio is right and any differences are due to chance. That assumption is called the null hypothesis.

χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E (Σ means "add up across all categories")

Worked example: flipping one coin 10 times

You expect 5 heads and 5 tails. You get 7 heads and 3 tails. Is the coin unfair?

  1. Subtract expected from observed for each row (O − E). Negative numbers are fine.
  2. Square each difference. This makes every value positive, so the misses above and below don't cancel out.
  3. Divide by the expected number for that row. A miss of 2 matters more when you only expected 5 than when you expected 500.
  4. Add the last column to get χ² = 1.6.
  5. Find the degrees of freedom: df = number of categories − 1 = 2 − 1 = 1.
  6. Look up the critical value for df = 1 at the 0.05 level in the table: 3.841.
  7. Compare. 1.6 is less than 3.841, so the difference is small enough to be explained by chance. We have no evidence the coin is unfair, even though 7 : 3 looked lopsided.

Albinism in a family

Albinism is a condition in which the body makes little or no melanin, the pigment that colors skin, hair, and eyes. The most common forms are recessive: a person has albinism only if they inherit two non-working alleles (aa), one from each parent. People with one working allele (Aa) have typical pigmentation but are carriers. In humans, albinism affects about 1 in 17,000 to 20,000 people worldwide. It also shows up in wild animals, which rarely survive long, because they stand out to predators and often have poor eyesight. You'll come back to this idea in the evolution and ecology labs.

The problem

Jessica has typical pigmentation. Her mother, Jill, has albinism. Jessica and her partner Tom, who also has albinism, are planning a family.

What genotypes and phenotypes could their children have, and in what ratios?

Use A for the allele for typical pigmentation and a for the albinism allele. Work through the steps in order. Each one gives you feedback.

Part 4 · Replaces Procedure 4

A two-gene (dihybrid) Punnett square

Now follow two genes at once. In peas, yellow seeds (Y) are dominant to green (y), and round seeds (R) are dominant to wrinkled (r). Mendel crossed two plants that were heterozygous for both genes: YyRr × YyRr.

Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment says the alleles of different genes separate into gametes independently of each other (as long as the genes are on different chromosomes). Getting Y tells you nothing about whether a gamete also gets R or r.

Questions

Question 4.1

How does your Punnett square show independent assortment? Point to one thing in the square that would look different if Y and R were always inherited together.

Question 4.2

Based on what you have learned about genetics, why is hybrid seed expensive to produce? And why won't seeds saved from a hybrid crop grow into plants just like their parents?

Here you'll trace a few easy-to-see traits through your family and try to work out which allele you got from which parent.

An honest note about human traits

Most human traits are not controlled by one gene with a dominant and a recessive allele. Earlobe shape, for example, is influenced by dozens of genes. In this part we will treat each trait as if it were a simple one-gene trait, because that is a useful model for practicing Mendelian logic. Each trait has a "Reality check" that tells you how well the model fits.

If your family doesn't fit the model, that is a real result, and it's evidence the trait isn't a simple one-gene trait. It tells you nothing about who is related to whom.

Choose a family

For each person, pick the trait they show. Choose "Don't know" for anyone you can't check. You need to fill in at least yourself.
